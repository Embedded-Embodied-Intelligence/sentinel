# WP10 notes — the design system and an exploratory QA through the browser

**Purpose.** WP10 rebuilds every screen to `docs/design/brief.md` on the same stack (ADR-0025, ADR-0027),
then drives the application as a manager would and fixes what that finds. Semantics are untouched.

**The design system as implemented.** Custom-property tokens with the brief's values for light and
dark mode, mobile first (min-width queries at 480, 768, 1024). Components are Jinja macros and
partials; about 25 inline SVG line icons, always `aria-hidden`, so a status always has a word.
`focus.html` is the shell without the sidebar for the wizard and a decision. A test holds the tokens'
contrast (4.5:1 text, 3:1 interface) and fails on a style nothing uses or a class no style defines.

**The Gantt.** An HTML column of focusable names beside an SVG with percentage x positions: names stay
in view when a phone scrolls the plot, and rows take the focus, which rows inside `role="img"` may not
(axe: nested-interactive). Flags are placed by a greedy tier assignment, tested never to overlap.

**Forecast wording.** Every chance is the NoReaction forecast of §11.4: a simulator run read 0 % for
weeks and finished on time, as Sentinel kept reacting. The pages say "if the plan stays as it is".

**Threats (v2.1.1).** Ranked by late share minus on-time share, from 10 points. On run 1 this names
tasks at 1 of 9 check-ins; the largest difference elsewhere is 7 points. The same tasks hold the critical
chain in late and on-time futures, so lateness most likely comes from their lengths. Which task is on the
chain says little about a project with one dominant chain.

**Threats (v2.1.2).** The tasks on the critical chain of at least half of the late futures, without those
on it in at least 95 % of all completed futures, by late share, at most three; at a chance of 0 % nothing
is left out as always critical. On run 1 this names 2 or 3 tasks at each of the 9 check-ins. Three limits.
The go-live's overall share averages 96.0 % (189 to 195 of 200) with a standard error of 1.4 points, so a
line at 95 % is crossed by chance, about 18 % of the time at that share: it tops the list on 16 October,
one future under the line. User training and acceptance tests are named at every check-in though they
decide on-time futures more often than late ones: the list says which tasks usually set the finish date,
not what makes the project late. And as `s_A = (1 - p) s_L + p s_O` for a chance `p`, at a chance of 5 % or
less (above 0 %) a task deciding every late future is left out, however rarely it decides on-time ones;
run 1 does not reach this (its lowest chance is 46 %).

**Threats (v2.1.3).** Duration sensitivity: for each unfinished task, Spearman's rank correlation (Spearman
1904) over the forecast's 200 futures between its realised duration (work in full-crew days, growth
included) and the finish day; at most three from 0.2, highest first. In the schedule-risk literature this
correlation is the cruciality index and the frequency rule it replaces the criticality index (on activity
sensitivity: Vanhoucke 2010, Omega 38(5), 359-370; check its definitions in the full text before citing).
The correlation is computed exactly on doubled mean ranks, so 0.2 is on the line whatever floats do; it
agrees with `scipy.stats.spearmanr` within 2e-16 on 2,000 random series with ties (a scratch check; scipy is
no dependency). On run 1 the pages name 1 to 3 tasks at each of the 9 check-ins, and "Login and accounts"
first on 8 October (0.38), after its growth of 4 days, which the two earlier rules did not name.

Two limits. The standard error at N = 200 is about 0.07, while most tasks of this project lie at 0.1 to 0.2:
at N = 4000, 5 of the 19 names at the check-ins reach 0.2, 4 of the 9 check-ins have no task there, and the
same state under another seed names 0 to 3 tasks. The signal of 8 October is real (0.41 at N = 4000, named at
40 of 40 seeds). And sensitivity is not leverage: it mixes how often a task is critical with how much it
varies. Lengthening a task by 2 days in the same 200 worlds moves the finish in 86 of 200 futures for "Login
and accounts" on 8 October (its length ranges from 5.3 to 20 days; it is on the critical chain of 27 of the
108 late futures), against 192 for the go-live and 180 for user training, which vary little and are not named.

**Threats (v2.1.4).** The same rule, line and limit on 1,000 futures whose first 200 are the forecast's
worlds: the seed and layout of the forecast give rollout i the same streams whatever the number of rollouts.
The standard error of a correlation near 0.2 falls from about 0.07 to about 0.03. The rollouts run in a
background job after a check-in (about 1 s on the 31 tasks of run 1), so the chance, the quantiles, the budget
outlook and the recommendation stay on the 200 worlds: all 17 moments of run 1 show the same figures, and the
two event logs differ only in wall times. A list is kept by the last event that can change a forecast, not by
`last_change`, which every recommendation and refusal moves. On run 1 (`scripts/threat_stability.py`,
`docs/qa/threat-stability.json`) the list changes at 6 of the 8 steps between check-ins instead of 8, and a
task enters or leaves without a related event 7 times instead of 14. That is less noise, not none: on one
state under other seeds, two lists differ in 60 of 81 pairs (79 of 81 at 200 futures), as 8 of the 15 names at
the check-ins lie within one standard error of the line. The signal of 8 October stands well above it at 0.37.

**The manager's report.** The pages used to link the WP8 cockpit, a research artefact. `report.html` and
`sim_report.html` are self-contained pages under the same wording check; the cockpit stays at the API.

**QA method.** `scripts/qa_run.py` starts `sentinel ui` on a fresh data directory and acts only
through the page (Playwright, Chromium); it reads the project's JSON in the same browser session for
its own books. A simulated team moves day by day: a task starts when its planned start has come, its
predecessors are done and its people are free; a partly present crew works at its share; real lengths
come from a seeded generator. Inputs: `docs/qa/zadania-portal.csv` (30 tasks), `docs/qa/zespol-portal.txt`
(11 people). Evidence: `docs/qa/shots/`, three JSON logs, `docs/qa/problems.md`.

**A methodological note.** The first version of the driver's team stopped a task as soon as anyone in
its crew was away, while the engine lets the others work at their share. That alone turned a calm
project into a 3 % one: the forecast was right about the wrong world. A QA driver of a forecasting
tool has to model the world as carefully as the tool does. Its second lesson: reading a chance as a
verdict is the main risk of misreading; the same project read 79 %, 46 % and 87 % within two weeks.
